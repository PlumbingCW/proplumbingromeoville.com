Sump Pump Repair Guide for Romeoville, IL Basements

A sump pump that fails during a storm is a basement flood waiting to happen. Start with the pit, the power and the float, since most failures come down to one of three things.

By the Romeoville Pro Plumbing team · Published October 29, 2026

When a sump pump quits, the pit overflows onto the basement floor, and it usually happens in the heaviest rain, when you can least afford it. Most pumps fail for ordinary reasons: no power, a stuck float, a clogged intake or a worn-out motor. You can check the first three in minutes.

Match the symptom to the likely cause

Sump pump symptoms and what to check
SymptomLikely causeFirst thing to try
Pump does nothing, pit fillsNo power, tripped GFCI, stuck float or dead motorCheck the outlet and reset the GFCI, then lift the float by hand
Pump hums but does not pumpJammed impeller or a failed capacitorUnplug it and clear the intake of grit
Pump runs constantlyFloat stuck up, stuck check valve, or a very high water tableCheck the float arm for obstruction and look for backflow when it stops
Pump turns on and off every few secondsCheck valve missing or failing, or pit too smallLook for water flowing back down the pipe after each cycle
Loud rattling or grindingDebris in the impeller or worn bearingsUnplug it and inspect the pump base
Pump runs but moves little waterFrozen or kinked discharge line, or clogged outletCheck where the discharge pipe leaves the house

A safe checklist before you touch anything

  1. Unplug the pump or switch off its breaker before putting a hand near the pit. Water and electricity do not mix.
  2. Look at the outlet and the GFCI. Reset it, then plug the pump back in and watch whether the float lifts as water rises.
  3. Pour a bucket of water into the pit. A working pump should kick on and drain it within a minute or two.
  4. Lift the float by hand while the pump is plugged in. If the motor starts, the float switch was sticking.
  5. Check the discharge pipe outside for ice, a crushed section or a closed valve.
  6. Listen for the check valve. A rush of water back into the pit as the pump stops means the valve is failing.

Do not work in a flooded pit with power on

If water is rising around outlets or cords, leave the basement and shut off the breaker from a dry spot. If you cannot reach the panel safely, call for help instead of wading in.

Common repairs and what they involve

A float switch swap is one of the quickest fixes. A failed check valve is also quick and prevents the short-cycling that wears out the motor. Cleaning silt from the pit helps the pump breathe. When the motor itself has failed, replacing the whole pump is usually more sensible than rebuilding it.

Repair or replace?

  • Repair when the pump is relatively new and the fault is a float, valve or cord.
  • Replace when the pump is old, rusted, noisy or runs rough after cleaning.
  • Replace when it has short-cycled for months, since the motor is likely worn.
  • Add a battery backup when the pump is the only protection you have against a flooded finished basement.

Our team handles sump pump repair and new installs, including backup systems that keep running when the power goes out. If water keeps coming despite a healthy pump, the problem is bigger than the pump, and basement waterproofing is the next conversation.

A pump that handles an ordinary storm may be overwhelmed by a single event like that. For you, it is an argument for a backup power source and a pump sized with room to spare.

Discharge rules are local. The city of Lockport's drainage page says a sump pump should discharge at least 10 feet from the property line, may tie into the public storm sewer only with permit approval, and may not be redirected to the sanitary sewer. Those are Lockport's rules, not Romeoville's. We found no sump pump rule on the Village's site, so ask the Village's Building Department before you reroute a discharge line.

The Village lists the 2014 State of Illinois Plumbing Code as its adopted plumbing code. As a safe habit, keep your sump discharge out of the sanitary sewer in any town, since clean rain water in the sanitary system can overload it during storms.

Keeping a pump healthy

  • Test it every spring and fall by pouring water into the pit.
  • Keep the pit clear of gravel, silt and stored items.
  • Confirm the discharge line points away from the house and is not frozen or kinked.
  • Plug it into a dedicated GFCI outlet, never an extension cord.
  • Replace a pump that has been running for most of a decade, even if it still works.
  • Install an alarm that sounds when the water level climbs too high.
