Copper Pipe for Residential Plumbing in Romeoville, IL

Copper has carried drinking water in American homes for generations. It is durable and it resists bacteria, but it is not immune to pinholes. Here is how to know what you have and what to do about it.

By the Romeoville Pro Plumbing team · Published October 29, 2026

Copper tubing is still one of the most common materials for water supply lines in houses built from the mid twentieth century onward. A copper line that is well installed and well matched to its water can run for decades. The trouble is that copper fails in a few predictable ways, and recognizing them early turns a ruined ceiling into a small repair.

Types K, L and M: what the letters mean

Rigid copper comes in three wall thicknesses. The letter tells you the thickness, and the thicker the wall, the more it costs and the longer it tends to last.

Copper pipe types for house plumbing
TypeWallWhere it is used
KThickestBuried service lines and the toughest jobs
LMediumThe usual choice for interior supply lines
MThinnestLighter-duty interior runs in many older and budget builds

Soft copper coil is also used outdoors and for some appliance connections. It bends without fittings, but it kinks if you push it too far.

How copper compares with the alternatives

Copper against other supply pipe
MaterialStrengthsWeaknesses
CopperLong life, handles heat, familiar to every plumberCan pit or pinhole, costs more, solder joints take skill
PEXFlexible, quick to install, tolerates freezing betterCan be damaged by sunlight, not for every location
CPVCInexpensive, no corrosionBrittle with age, can crack at fittings
Galvanized steelFound only in older homesRusts from inside, restricts flow and is usually replaced

No material wins everywhere. Copper often remains the better pick for exposed runs, the water heater connections and any place where heat and durability matter. PEX makes sense for long runs that need to snake through finished walls.

How copper pipe fails

  • Pinhole leaks from pitting corrosion, where small spots eat through the wall from the inside
  • Erosion corrosion where water moves too fast, often near elbows and the hot water side
  • Failed solder joints, particularly where flux was left behind or the joint was not fully heated
  • Freeze damage, with a split along the length of the tube
  • Outside corrosion from contact with damp concrete, wet insulation or leaking neighbors

One pinhole does not mean the whole house needs new pipe. Several pinholes in different rooms within a short period do point to a system-wide problem, and that is when repiping starts to be a cost-effective conversation.

Signs copper is wearing out

  • Green or blue crust at joints and along the tube
  • Tiny spray or a fine mist from a pipe in a basement or crawl space
  • Water stains that return after a ceiling patch dries
  • Blue-green stains on sinks and tubs
  • Falling pressure that does not trace to a fixture

Romeoville note: what the Village's water report says about home plumbing

The Village of Romeoville's drinking water quality report lists its 2024 lead and copper sampling. The copper 90th percentile result was 0.145 ppm against an action level of 1.3 ppm, with no sites above the limit. Lead was 1.44 ppb against an action level of 15 ppb. For you, those results show the water meeting the federal limits at the sampled homes in that year.

The report also states that the Village's drinking water system contains no lead water mains or service lines, and that lead in drinking water comes primarily from service lines and home plumbing. It names corrosion of household plumbing as the likely source of both lead and copper. That puts the focus on the pipes inside your house, which the report says you share responsibility for.

The report recommends flushing your pipes for several minutes before drinking if the water has sat unused. If your home has older copper with lead solder or brass fixtures, that habit matters most. The Village's Building Department enforces the 2014 State of Illinois Plumbing Code, which it lists as its adopted plumbing code, so ask about permits before any repiping.

Repair or repipe: a plain decision guide

  1. One leak, pipe otherwise sound, house under thirty years old: repair the section.
  2. Two or three leaks in separate places within a year or two: have the plumber inspect and test the system before repairing again.
  3. Green crust spread across many joints or walls that feel rough inside when cut open: plan a repipe.
  4. Frozen and split copper after a hard winter: repair the break and insulate the run.
  5. Mixed galvanized and copper with poor flow: repipe the galvanized portion at minimum.

For an isolated failure, our burst pipe repair team cuts out the damaged section and sweats in a new piece or uses a press fitting. For a house that keeps springing leaks, repiping replaces the failing runs, in copper or in PEX, depending on the walls and the budget.

Soldering, press fittings and what to expect

Sweating a copper joint means heating the pipe and fitting until solder is drawn into the gap. Done properly, it is permanent. Press fittings use a crimping tool and a rubber seal, which is quicker in tight spots and where an open flame is a risk.

Either approach is acceptable when done by a licensed plumber. What matters is clean, square cuts, proper reaming and a fully dry pipe, because water in the line makes soldering fail.

Age, neighborhood and what you might find

Pipe type tracks the era of construction. Homes in older streets of Crest Hill are more likely to mix copper with older materials, while newer subdivisions in Plainfield are more likely to have PEX or newer copper. Those are general patterns, not rules. The reliable way to know is to look at the pipe where it enters at the meter and at the water heater.
